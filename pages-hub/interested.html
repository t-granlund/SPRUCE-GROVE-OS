just curious · the ninety-second story

A machine that builds,
a club that teaches,
a lineage from 1727.

Spruce Grove OS is a free, open-source machine that builds software the way a good apprentice works: you describe the job out loud, it thinks in the open, checks its own work, and shows you everything while it happens. It runs on your accounts, on your computer, and it is yours — there is no monthly toll on your own productivity.

The Leather Apron Club is the people around the machine. A council of twelve that meets on Friday evenings in Bentonville, Arkansas, and does what Benjamin Franklin's circle did in Philadelphia in 1727: neighbors pool their skills and build what the world isn't offering yet. His circle's answer to needing books was the first subscription library in America. This circle's answer to needing software is teaching every neighbor to own their own.

beat · one

It is already working

A drone outfit's lead pipeline — spoken brief to live site, every serious step gated by a human, tested the way a customer uses it. A barbershop's shopfront — the shop owns it, the club maintains it. Both are public pages you can open right now.

beat · two

The way of working has already traveled

The method behind the machine — honest requirements, the machine doing the heavy lifting, people gating what matters — went from a pilot of ten-odd people to seventy-five thousand inside one of the largest companies on earth. One of the people it freed had no college degree and became a director of the work at thirty-five.

beat · three

It is free, and it will stay free

Apache-2.0 — the license that makes attribution travel with the work and shields the people who use it. The club's words are CC BY: take the creed, credit the table. The puppy the grove was born from — Code Puppy, by Michael Pfaffenberger — is named in the receipt that ships inside every copy.

beat · four

Where the money goes

The machine is free, and the club sells nothing. Where profit exists at all — a video, a sponsorship, whatever comes — it is ledgered in public and donated to the 43 Institute, Ken Block's foundation: a 501(c)(3) creating pathways for underserved, highly driven "motivated misfits" in action sports, motorsports, and the creative arts. Racing and rally, skateboards and BMX, art and music and print — the kids who love things loudly and lack the means to chase them. We recognize the species: our own proof is a woman with no college degree who became a director of the work at thirty-five — a motivated misfit handed a pathway. Theirs is a kid in a screen-printing class whose art project became DC Shoes. Talent is evenly distributed; means are not. The machine stays free; the value flows to the misfits; the ledger stays public. 43i.org
